Cloudflare needs a hard spending cap

2026-10-15 · Øyvind Ødegård

Everyone has started creating new apps and services nowadays, now that coding is solved. Follow that link before you quote me on it.

Yet any good idea still needs to be hosted, and the big clouds (AWS, Azure, GCP) have the aura of being big and bulky. Now Cloudflare comes along as a fresh new player, offering agent setup with Skills and MCP servers "so your agent can seamlessly build on the Cloudflare platform", and positioning itself as an agent-first cloud provider. I like it. This site and my side projects live there.

The catch is that an agent will deploy a loop just as happily as a feature, and on usage-based billing a loop is not a crash. Nothing errors. The meter just runs, and nobody is watching at 3am. This year has been full of people finding out the hard way. In August Justin Schroeder woke up to a $8,846 Cloudflare bill and summed it up: "We found out by getting the bill."

In June a two-person team was billed $5,864 because "an LLM coding agent on our project shipped a change that rewrote the same data every few minutes": about 5 billion D1 writes from a workers.dev Worker. Their alert emails landed in spam, and the first signal was the bank debit. "Right now there's no good way to defend against an agent going off the rails between two human reviews," they wrote.

In September a non-developer building with Claude Code got a four-figure bill for a missing index that made every crawler hit on a staging site scan 306,000 rows. Cloudflare's default $10 budget alert showed $0.00 the whole month. "When I have a bug, I get an invoice with no ceiling on it."

Neither of them did anything exotic. Something looped, nobody was looking, and the first alarm was the invoice. That will happen a lot more when an agent writes the code and the deploy is one tool call away.

To be fair to Cloudflare, it is closing the gap. There is a Billable Usage dashboard, budget alerts are now on by default, and on 2 October it said it is "prototyping hard spending caps now, with early availability in Q4 2026". I hope that lands soon. Until then an alert is a smoke detector, and what you want at 3am is a sprinkler.

So I built one. Controlflare is a monthly cap per Cloudflare account. Every five minutes it estimates the month's spend across Workers, D1, R2, KV and Durable Object requests, and when the estimate crosses your number it pauses your Workers: the workers.dev URL, custom domains, routes and cron triggers come off, and so do the D1, KV, R2 and Durable Object bindings. Each is saved first, so a resume puts it back, and it never resumes on its own. Your Cloudflare API token stays in your own account, held by a small open-source agent you deploy there.

It would have paused that agent's runaway writer within hours, and it reads the same analytics that showed the Claude Code user the real numbers while the alert said $0.00. Plenty of this year's other horror stories are the same kind:

  • Thunderbit was billed $38,772 when a bug in its Chrome extension had about 200,000 installs calling its Worker over and over.
  • Little Bear Apps was billed $4,868 when a cron Worker kept reprocessing the same batch: 3.45 billion D1 writes in four days. Cloudflare waived it.
  • CloudPrinters.sa was billed about $2,078 for a token refresh retry loop that rewrote one account's history some 2 billion times.
  • blackdiary was billed $564 for a query without an index on every request.

Most of those would have crossed a $50 cap within hours. A pause takes the Worker offline, of course, but that is the trade a cap makes: a broken site for an evening instead of an invoice with no ceiling.

What it cannot stop is a Durable Object alarm loop, and those have produced some of the biggest bills of the year. In April, in the middle of Cloudflare's Agents Week, a solo founder found that their AI agent on Durable Objects had scheduled a new health check alarm on every wake-up, across more than 60 preview deployments. Zero users, around 930 billion row reads a day at the peak, and a $34,895 invoice. "Running Agents Week while that gap exists is not a neutral decision," they wrote.

And last week @shmily7 got one for $10,811. They had vibe coded a multi-agent experiment on Cloudflare's Sandbox, shelved it, and left it deployed. A Codex commit at the end of August had planted a Durable Object alarm that rescheduled itself; it woke up 23 days later and ran up six trillion reads and writes. Cloudflare agreed to refund it after an escalation. Their own line, translated, sums up the era: if AI is the magic wand, Cloudflare is where the magic lands.

The alarm runs inside the object, and short of redeploying or deleting your code, nothing outside it can cancel it. Only Cloudflare can cap that, which is why I still want the native one. Until it ships, Controlflare is a safety net with holes in it: the spend is an estimate, not your invoice, and the disclaimer lists every hole. Keep Cloudflare's budget alerts on as well.

Full disclosure, it is mine. It is 30 days free, then $1 a month.
